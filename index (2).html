Physics Notes

1 · Physics of LED emission

An LED is a p–n junction made of a direct-bandgap semiconductor. Forward bias lowers the potential barrier so electrons from the n-side and holes from the p-side flow into the junction region and recombine. Each recombination releases the bandgap energy as one photon:

E_g = h f = hc / λ

An electron crossing the junction gains energy eV from the supply. Light only starts to appear once this energy reaches the gap, so at the turn-on (threshold) voltage:

e V_th ≈ E_g = h f

Wider-gap materials (blue, InGaN) need more voltage than narrower-gap ones (red, AlGaInP) — exactly the trend in the data: 1.908 V → 2.318 V → 2.650 V.

2 · Deriving h from the slope

Start from energy balance at turn-on and solve for the voltage:

e V_th = h f ⟹ V_th = (h / e) · f

This is a straight line y = m x + b with y = V_th, x = f and slope m = h/e. A least-squares fit gives:

m = Σ(f − f̄)(V − V̄) / Σ(f − f̄)² h = m · e (e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)

The intercept b should be ≈ 0 V; a small non-zero value signals systematic offsets (series resistance, contact potential, visibility criterion).

3 · Sources of experimental error
  • Thermal broadening. Carriers are spread over about kT ≈ 25.9 meV at 300 K, so emission is not a single frequency. The spectrum has a width of roughly 1.8 kT (≈ 20–30 nm) and the "colour" frequency used is only the nominal peak/dominant value.
  • Human visual threshold. V_th is judged by eye. Because current rises exponentially with V, the glow becomes visible a bit below or above the true onset depending on room light, dark adaptation and LED brightness. Eye sensitivity also differs strongly across red, green and blue.
  • Nominal frequencies. Values like 4.6, 5.6 and 6.4 ×10¹⁴ Hz are catalogue wavelengths, and the real peak varies between batches and shifts with temperature.
  • Series resistance and heating. I·R_s inside the LED adds to the measured voltage, and junction heating lowers the bandgap slightly during the measurement.
  • Meter resolution and random scatter. The ±0.01 V spread across the five trials sets the statistical uncertainty. With only three data points the fit uncertainty is large.
